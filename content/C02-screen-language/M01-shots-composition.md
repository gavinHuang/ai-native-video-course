## Learn

### Shot size
Shot size decides what the viewer sees, and so what the viewer thinks is important. See the diagram for the 6 main sizes, plus the **full shot** (head to feet, between WS and MS). Rules:
- Start a scene wide enough to show where we are (establishing shot), unless confusion is the goal.
- Move closer as emotion grows.
- Use the closest shots rarely. They lose power if overused.

![Six shot sizes on the same subject](fig:shot-sizes.svg)

| Abbr. | Full name | Typical framing |
|---|---|---|
| EWS | Extreme Wide Shot (also Extreme Long Shot, ELS) | The subject is tiny; the setting dominates |
| WS | Wide Shot (also Long Shot, LS) | Whole body with plenty of surroundings |
| FS | Full Shot | Head to feet, filling the frame |
| MS | Medium Shot (also Mid Shot) | Waist up |
| MCU | Medium Close-Up | Chest or shoulders up |
| CU | Close-Up | The face fills most of the frame |
| ECU | Extreme Close-Up (also XCU) | A single detail: eyes, mouth, hands |

### Camera height and angle
- **Eye level:** neutral.
- **Low angle** (camera below, looking up): power, threat, heroism.
- **High angle** (camera above, looking down): weakness, being watched.
- **Overhead / top shot:** pattern, distance, a "god's view".
- **Dutch angle** (tilted horizon): unease. Use rarely.
- **Over-the-shoulder (OTS):** dialogue; connects two people.
- **Point of view (POV):** we see what the character sees.

![Camera heights and angles, as seen in the frame](fig:camera-angles.svg)

### Composition
- **Rule of thirds:** place the subject or eyes on the lines that divide the frame in thirds.
- **Lead room:** leave space in the direction of a look or a movement.
- **Headroom:** a small space above the head in MS; cut the forehead a little in a tight CU.
- **Depth layers:** foreground, middle ground, background. Something in the foreground (a glass, a branch) makes the image deep.
- **Leading lines:** roads, rails, and walls lead the eye to the subject.
- **Symmetry:** order, control, sometimes threat.
- **Negative space:** empty area that makes a subject feel alone or calm.

![Composition rules, one frame each](fig:composition.svg)

### Shots by content
Shot size says how close; these names say what is in the frame:
- **Single:** one person. **Two-shot:** two people in one frame, which shows their relation. **Group shot.**
- **Insert:** a close detail of an object or action (a hand on a key, a message on a phone). Inserts carry information and make cuts easier.
- **Cutaway:** a shot of something outside the main action (a clock, a passer-by, the street). It shows time or context, and hides a jump in the main action.
- **Reaction shot:** a listener's face. Reactions often carry the emotion of a dialogue more than the speaker.
Plan inserts, cutaways, and reactions on purpose. In AI production they are cheap to make and they solve many editing problems (C02·M04).

### Where the viewer looks
The eye goes first to faces (especially eyes), then to the brightest area, the area of highest contrast, sharp focus, warm colors, and movement. Use this to lead attention: put the important thing where these meet, and keep competing bright or moving areas out of the frame. When you review a generated frame, ask: where did my eye go first? If it is not the subject, change the light, the focus, or the composition.

### Aspect ratio
The frame shape changes composition. 16:9 for YouTube and TV; 9:16 for Shorts, Reels, TikTok; 2.39:1 for "cinema" look; 1:1 or 4:5 for some feeds. Compose for the delivery ratio from the start. A model that generates 16:9 and is then cropped to 9:16 loses the composition.

### Safe areas
Platforms put buttons, captions, and profile names over the picture, mostly at the bottom and the right side of vertical video. Keep faces, text, and products inside the safe area of the delivery format (C10·M03 gives the exact margins). For TV, keep text inside the title-safe area (about 90 % of the frame).

### Why this matters for AI
A prompt without shot size and angle gives the model's default: usually a centered medium shot at eye level. Every professional shot list names size, angle, and composition. Your prompts must too.

## Worked example

**Scene:** a delivery rider arrives at night at an old apartment block. Goal: tension, then relief.

| # | Size | Angle | Composition | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | EWS | High, overhead drone | Rider small in lower third; wet street as leading line | Place, isolation |
| 2 | MS | Low, slight | Rider on left third, lead room to building | Determination |
| 3 | ECU | Eye level | Finger on a broken doorbell, shallow focus | Tension detail |
| 4 | CU | Eye level | Face in the doorway light, right third | Reaction |
| 5 | MCU | OTS from the rider | Old woman opens the door, warm light | Relief |

Prompt for shot 3: "Extreme close-up, eye level, a gloved finger presses a cracked, rusty doorbell button, shallow depth of field, rain drops on the metal, cold blue night light, 9:16 vertical."

## Lab

**Deliverable:** `C02/M01/`, with a shot table and 6 generated frames.

1. Choose a 2–3 minute scene from a film you like.
2. Make a shot table: number, size, angle, composition, purpose (at least 15 shots).
3. Write 6 prompts for a new scene of your own, each with a different size and angle.
4. Generate them with any image model. Keep the same character description in all 6.
5. Score each frame: did the model give the size, the angle, and the composition you asked for? (yes/partly/no)

**Result checklist**
- [ ] Shot table with 15+ rows and purposes.
- [ ] 6 frames, each with its prompt.
- [ ] A score table and 3 sentences on which terms the model followed best.

## Model answer

Typical finding: models follow shot size well (wide, close-up), follow "low angle" and "high angle" most of the time, and often ignore "rule of thirds" and "lead room" (they center the subject). Fixes: describe the position ("subject on the left third of the frame, looking right, empty space on the right"), or give a reference image or a rough sketch as the composition guide. Extreme close-ups of hands still show defects in some models; keep them short or use a real insert shot.
